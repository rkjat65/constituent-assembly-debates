Fundamental Rights

Theme · rights architecture

A right becomes constitutional only after the Assembly answers four different questions.

What liberty or equality is being promised? Is the claim enforceable in a court? What restrictions may the State impose? And what remedy exists when public power violates the guarantee? By May 1947 a fifth question is repeatedly visible too: when a hard case exposes defects in a clause, should the House revise it immediately or send it back for specialised reconsideration?

Hard cases reveal the boundaries of rights

Forced labour collides with compulsory service; religious liberty collides with coercion, family authority and social reform; cultural rights collide with common-language and educational policy. Some clauses are sent back rather than settled prematurely.

Five analytical lenses

1 · Substance2 · Enforceability3 · Restrictions4 · Remedies5 · Referral

Substance: what liberty, equality or protection is promised? Enforceability: may a citizen compel compliance through a court? Restrictions: what public interests justify limiting the right? Remedies: which institution can make the guarantee effective? Referral: when does a difficult consequence require another round of committee work?

Keeping these layers separate prevents a common reading error: treating every constitutional aspiration as if it had the same legal status, the same restrictions and the same enforcement mechanism.
